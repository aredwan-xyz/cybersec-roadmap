# Lesson 2: Protect yourself first

> **In one sentence:** A handful of habits, set up once, stop most of the attacks that ordinary people face.

**Time:** about 15 minutes · **You need:** a browser

## The big idea

Imagine one key that opens your house, your car and your office. If someone copies it, they get everything at once. That is what reusing one password on many websites does.

Websites get broken into often, and lists of email addresses and passwords leak. Criminals then try those pairs on other sites, such as email and banking. This is called **credential stuffing** ("credentials" means the details you log in with). If you reused the password, they get in. Many account break-ins happen this way, with no clever hacking at all.

The fix is not to remember dozens of clever passwords. Nobody can. The fix is to let tools do the remembering, and to add a second lock so a stolen password alone is not enough.

The habits below take an afternoon to set up. Together they protect your accounts from strangers, your files from loss, and your ability to get back to normal when something goes wrong.

## How it works

- **Password manager.** An app that creates and stores a different strong password for every site, locked behind one main password. It fills them in for you, and will not fill them into a fake look-alike site. Options include Bitwarden, 1Password, and those built into Apple, Google and Microsoft devices.
- **Unique, long passwords.** Length matters more than odd symbols. Let the manager generate them. For your one main password, use four or more random words.
- **Passkeys.** A newer replacement for passwords. Your device keeps a secret key and unlocks it with your fingerprint, face or PIN. A passkey only works on the real site it was made for, so a fake site cannot steal it. Use them wherever offered.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA).** A second proof on top of your password: a code from an app, a security key, or a prompt on your phone. Text-message codes are the weakest kind, but far better than nothing. Start with your email, because it can reset every other account.
- **Updates.** Updates fix vulnerabilities attackers already know about. Turn on automatic updates everywhere.
- **Backups, the 3-2-1 rule.** Keep 3 copies of important files, on 2 different kinds of storage, with 1 copy somewhere else, such as a cloud service. Backups save you from theft, broken devices and ransomware.
- **Device lock.** Use a PIN or password on your phone and computer, and set the screen to lock itself after a short time.

## In real life

Someone uses the same password for a small online forum and their email. The forum is breached and its password list is sold. An automated tool tries that email and password on the big email services, and it works.

From the email account, the criminal clicks "forgot password" on shopping and social media sites, and the reset links arrive in the inbox they now control. One reused password became many stolen accounts. A unique password, or MFA on the email account, would have stopped it at the first step.

## Try it

1. Turn on multi-factor authentication for your main email account. Look in its security settings. Use an authenticator app or passkey if offered.
2. Check that automatic updates are on for your phone and computer, and install anything waiting.
3. Install a password manager on one device, save your email password in it, then change one reused password to a new one the manager generates.

## Key words

- **Password manager**: an app that creates, stores and fills in your passwords.
- **Credential stuffing**: trying leaked usernames and passwords on many other websites.
- **Passkey**: a login that uses a secret key on your device instead of a password, and only works on the real site.
- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**: needing two or more kinds of proof to log in.
- **Ransomware**: malware that locks your files and demands payment to unlock them.

## Check yourself

**1. Why is reusing one password on many sites dangerous?**

- A. If one site leaks it, criminals can use it to get into your other accounts
- B. Websites can see each other's passwords
- C. Reused passwords expire faster

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Leaked password lists are tried automatically on other sites. Unique passwords keep a leak contained.

</details>

**2. Which account should get multi-factor authentication first?**

- A. A game you play once a month
- B. A newsletter subscription
- C. Your main email account

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Your email can reset the passwords of almost every other account.

</details>

**3. What does the "1" in the 3-2-1 backup rule mean?**

- A. Back up once a year
- B. Keep one copy in a different place from the others
- C. Use only one backup app

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** An off-site copy survives fire, theft or ransomware that hits everything at home.

</details>
