# Lesson 3: Spot scams and phishing

> **In one sentence:** Most scams work by rushing or pressuring you into acting before you think, so slowing down and checking through a separate route defeats most of them.

**Time:** about 15 minutes · **You need:** a browser

## The big idea

A con artist does not pick your lock. They knock wearing a convincing uniform, say there is a gas leak, and ask to come in quickly. You open the door yourself.

Online scams work the same way. **Social engineering** means tricking people, rather than computers, into giving something away: a password, a payment, a code, or access. It is cheap, and it works even against well-protected systems.

**Phishing** is the most common form. It is a message pretending to be from someone you trust, such as your bank, a delivery company or your boss. It tries to get you to click a link, log in to a fake page, or send money. Phishing by text message is often called **smishing**, and by phone call **vishing**.

You do not need to be an expert to beat these tricks. Recognise the pressure, then check the claim yourself using a number, app or website you already know is real.

## How it works

Scams lean on a few feelings that make people act fast:

- **Urgency**: "Your account will be closed in 24 hours."
- **Authority**: "This is the fraud team," or a message that seems to come from your manager.
- **Fear**: "Unusual login detected," "you owe tax," "a warrant is out."
- **Reward**: a prize, a refund, an easy job offer.

When you notice any of these, stop. Then check:

- **The sender.** Look at the actual email address, not just the display name, which anyone can set. Caller numbers can be faked too.
- **The link.** On a computer, hover over it to see where it goes. Read the name just before `.com`, `.org` or similar. `paypal.com.account-check.net` belongs to `account-check.net`, not PayPal.
- **The request.** Banks do not ask for your password or one-time codes. Real companies do not ask for payment in gift cards.

The safest habit: **never use the contact details inside the message**. Use the official app, type the address yourself, or call the number on your card.

**If you clicked or replied:** act quickly, without panic. Change that account's password on the real site, and anywhere you reused it. Turn on MFA. If you gave payment details, call your bank on its official number. Report the message, and tell your IT team if it was a work account. Being fooled is not stupid; these tricks are built by professionals.

## In real life

In July 2020, attackers phoned employees at Twitter and posed as the company's own IT staff. They talked some of them into entering their login details on a fake site. That gave the attackers access to internal tools.

They used it to take over well-known accounts, including Barack Obama's and Elon Musk's, and posted a scam asking followers to send bitcoin. No clever code broke in. A phone call and some pressure did.

## Try it

1. Take the free [Google Jigsaw phishing quiz](https://phishingquiz.withgoogle.com). It shows realistic example emails and explains the clues.
2. Find one scam message in your spam folder. Without clicking anything, note its pressure tricks and real sender address.
3. Find your bank's official phone number from its app or your card, and save it in your contacts as the number you will use to check any "bank" call.

## Key words

- **Social engineering**: tricking people into giving away information, access or money.
- **Phishing**: a fake message pretending to be from someone you trust.
- **Smishing**: phishing by text message (SMS).
- **Vishing**: phishing by voice call.
- **Spoofing**: faking where a message or call appears to come from.
- **Domain**: the main name in a web address, such as `example.com`.

## Check yourself

**1. A text asks you to pay a small fee through a link to release your parcel. What is safest?**

- A. Pay, because the amount is small
- B. Reply asking if it is real
- C. Ignore the link and check the delivery company's official app yourself

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Checking through a route you already trust avoids the scam. Paying hands over your card details.

</details>

**2. Which web address actually belongs to Example Bank?**

- A. `login.examplebank.com`
- B. `examplebank.com.secure-login.net`
- C. `examplebank-support.help`

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** The owner is the name just before the ending: `examplebank.com`. B belongs to `secure-login.net`, and C is a different domain.

</details>

**3. Someone calling from "your bank" asks you to read out the code they just texted you. What should you do?**

- A. Read it out, since they know your name
- B. Hang up and call the bank yourself on its official number
- C. Read out only half the code

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** The caller is probably using the code to log in as you. Call back on a number you trust.

</details>
